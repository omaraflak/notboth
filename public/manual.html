Building a Computer from One Gate

What to make, in what order, and why each part earns its place. This manual does not hand you circuits. It tells you which piece is next and what job it has to do — the working out is the part worth having.

PartsFive, plus an appendix
Stages39

Ends withA program that finds primes

Before you switch anything on

A NAND gate answers one question: are both of my inputs on? — and then says the opposite. That is the whole of it. It has no memory, no arithmetic, no notion of a number. From that, in thirty-nine moves, you can build a machine that runs software you wrote in a language you designed.

This works because NAND is functionally complete: every logical function that can exist can be written as some arrangement of NANDs. You do not have to take that on faith for long — the first three stages prove it by construction.

Three habits are worth adopting from the start. Build small. A component should do one nameable thing. If you cannot name it in one word, it is two components. Test before you move on. Every chip gets a truth table before it gets used, without exception; a wrong wire inside a four-gate part is invisible from the outside and will resurface three layers later as “my CPU computes nonsense”, at which point you will have a hundred suspects instead of four. Never reopen a solved problem. Once a chip passes its tests, it is furniture. Use it and forget how it works.

Each stage below gives you the name of the part, its inputs and outputs, why the machine needs it, and how you will know it works. The circuit inside is yours to find. When you are stuck, the answer is almost always a part you already built.

I

The gates

Ten parts, none of them larger than a handful of NANDs. By the end of this Part you will be thinking less about gates and more about operations, which is where the rest of the build happens.

1.01Needsnothing

Not

Not(in) → out

Negation. Start here because everything else leans on it, and because it is the smallest possible proof that one gate is enough: you have a part with two inputs and you need a part with one, so the first thing you must work out is what to do with the spare.

Once this exists you can stop thinking of NAND as “not-and” and start thinking of it as raw material.

Done whenTwo vectors pass: 0 gives 1, 1 gives 0.
1.02Needs1.01

And

And(a, b) → out

“Both.” The most direct question you can ask about two signals, and the one the ALU will lean on hardest. This one is nearly free — you have a gate that computes the opposite of it and a part that reverses things.

The lesson here is not the circuit, it is the reflex: when a part looks like something you already have with a twist, it usually is.

Done whenAll four input combinations pass.
1.03Needs1.01

Or

Or(a, b) → out

“Either.” Harder than it looks, because NAND is fundamentally an and-shaped gate and you are asking it for an or. The way through is De Morgan’s observation: saying “a or b” is the same as saying “not both of them are off.” Read that sentence again with your parts list in hand and the circuit falls out of it.

This is the first time the manual asks you to change the shape of a question rather than build a bigger circuit. You will do it constantly from here.

Done whenAll four combinations pass, and you can say out loud why it works.
1.04Needs1.01–1.03

Xor

Xor(a, b) → out

“Different.” This one earns its keep twice over. In Part II it turns out to be binary addition — 1 plus 1 is 0, carry the 1, which is exactly what Xor says about two bits. And inverted, it is equality, which is how your machine will eventually compare two numbers.

Xor is the first part with real internal structure. If it comes out at four or five NANDs you are in good company.

Done whenAll four combinations pass. Keep this one close; you will use it constantly.
1.05Needs1.01–1.03

Mux

Mux(a, b, sel) → out

A multiplexer: two data inputs, one selector, and the output is whichever input the selector points at. Read that again as a sentence about software and it says out = sel ? b : a. This is an if, in copper.

If you build only one part from this Part with real care, make it this one. A computer is very largely muxes. Choosing which number the ALU operates on is a mux. Deciding whether a register keeps its value or takes a new one is a mux. Choosing whether the program counter steps forward or jumps is a mux. Almost every decision your machine ever makes is this chip, wearing a different label.

Watch outTest all four combinations of a and b against both settings of sel — eight vectors, not four. A mux that only works when the data happens to agree is a classic silent failure.
1.06Needs1.01–1.03

DMux

DMux(in, sel) → a, b

The mirror image: one input, two outputs, and the selector decides which one receives it. The other output goes quiet.

Where a mux gathers, a demux routes. You will need this the moment you build memory, because storing a value at an address means delivering a write signal to exactly one storage cell out of many and to none of the others. Addressing is demultiplexing.

Done whenBoth selector settings route correctly and the unselected output is reliably 0.
1.07Needs1.01–1.05

Widen everything to sixteen

Not16(in[16]) → out[16]   ·   And16, Or16, Mux16

Your machine will work on words, not bits — sixteen bits at a time is a good size: big enough to address a useful amount of memory, small enough to wire without losing your mind. So each of the gates you just built needs a wide sibling that applies it to every bit position independently.

There is no cleverness here and that is the point: this is the first stage that is purely mechanical, and it is where you will be grateful that pins have widths and wires can carry a whole bus. Sixteen copies of a part, side by side, sharing a control signal.

Note what Mux16 is: a single selector bit choosing between two entire numbers. That is the shape of nearly every control decision to come.

Watch outTest the ends and the middle, not just 0000… and 1111…. A bus wired one bit out of alignment passes both of those and nothing else.
1.08Needs1.03

Or16Way

Or16Way(in[16]) → out

“Is anything at all switched on in here?” A single bit summarising a whole bus.

It looks like a throwaway utility. It is not: this is how your machine answers “is this number zero?”, and that question is the entire basis of conditional branching. Sixteen wide because a number is sixteen wide — invert this chip’s output and you have the ALU’s zr exactly, with nothing left over to build. Every if and every while in every program you ever write on this machine will come down to a chip like this one looking at a bus and shrugging.

Done whenAll-zeros gives 0, and each single bit set on its own gives 1.
1.09Needs1.05, 1.07

Mux4Way16 and Mux8Way16

Mux4Way16(a..d[16], sel[2]) → out[16]  ·  Mux8Way16(a..h[16], sel[3]) → out[16]

Choosing between two things is not enough. Build the four-way first, then the eight-way from it: two selector bits and four candidate words, then three bits and eight. One winner either way.

Do not invent a new mechanism for this. Build it out of the two-way mux you already have, in layers — and notice while you do that a selector bit is just an address bit. That realisation is most of Part III.

Done whenEvery selector value picks the right input, in both sizes. Feed each input a distinct recognisable value so a mis-order is obvious.
1.10Needs1.06

DMux4Way and DMux8Way

DMux4Way(in, sel[2]) → a..d  ·  DMux8Way(in, sel[3]) → a..h

The routing counterpart, and your address decoder. Two bits in, one of four lines raised and three held down; then three bits and one of eight.

When you build RAM you will hand it an address and expect exactly one cell to react. This chip is what makes that true. Build it the same way as the wide mux: in layers, out of the part you already have.

One thing worth being explicit about, because the pair looks lopsided next to each other: the muxes in the stage above are sixteen bits wide and these are one bit. That is not an omission. A mux carries data — it chooses between whole numbers, so it is as wide as a number. A demux carries a decision — it routes a single write signal to one destination out of several. There is no DMux4Way16 because nothing in the machine needs to send a whole word down one of four roads; it needs to tell one register out of four to listen.

Done whenEvery selector value raises exactly one output and no other.
II

Arithmetic

Six parts that turn logic into mathematics. This Part ends with the single most important chip in the machine, and it is a good deal less frightening than its reputation.

2.01Needs1.02, 1.04

HalfAdder

HalfAdder(a, b) → sum, carry

Add two bits. There are only four cases and you can write them on your hand: 0+0, 0+1, 1+0, and 1+1 — which is two, which does not fit in one bit, which is why there is a second output.

Look at the two output columns separately and you will find you built both of these chips in Part I. That is the whole stage: arithmetic is not a new kind of thing, it is logic looked at from a different angle.

Done whenAll four cases produce the right sum and carry.
2.02Needs2.01, 1.03

FullAdder

FullAdder(a, b, c) → sum, carry

Add three bits. The third is the carry arriving from the column to the right — the same thing you do by hand when you add 17 and 25 and put a little 1 above the tens column.

This is the unit cell of all your arithmetic, so build it out of half adders rather than from scratch. Getting the carry-out right is the fiddly bit: think about when a column produces a carry rather than trying to draw the circuit first.

Done whenAll eight input combinations pass. Write all eight; there is no excuse at this size.
2.03Needs2.02

Add16

Add16(a[16], b[16]) → out[16]

Sixteen full adders in a row, each one’s carry-out feeding the next one’s carry-in. Now your machine can add two numbers, which — with the negation trick two stages from here — means it can also subtract, compare, and count.

What happens to the carry off the far end is your decision to make. Most designs simply drop it and let numbers wrap around; that is what “overflow” means and it is a perfectly respectable choice.

This design is called ripple carry because the carry has to walk the whole width before the answer is final. It is the slowest adder anyone builds and it is the right one to build first. If you later want it faster, the search term is carry-lookahead, and swapping it in is one command once your test vectors exist.

Watch outTest the carry chain deliberately: something plus one that ripples the whole way, and the largest possible pair, which should wrap to a small number.
2.04Needs2.03

Inc16

Inc16(in[16]) → out[16]

Add one. You could of course use the adder with a hard-wired 1, and you may — but adding one is such a common operation that it is worth having its own name in your library.

The reason is Part III. A program counter spends almost its entire life doing exactly this, once per instruction, forever. Naming the operation now means the counter reads as what it is instead of as a special case of addition.

Done whenZero becomes one, and the maximum value wraps to zero.
2.05Needs2.03, 2.04, 1.07

Negative numbers, and why subtraction is free

no new chip — a decision

This stage builds nothing. It settles a question, and the answer shapes everything after it.

You need negative numbers, and you emphatically do not want to build a second machine for subtracting. The convention that saves you is two’s complement: negate a number by inverting every bit and adding one. Under that convention a - b is literally a + (-b), the adder you already have does both jobs, and comparison becomes subtraction followed by a glance at the result.

Convince yourself it works before moving on. Take a small number, invert it, add one, then add the original back and check that you land on zero. Do it with pencil and paper — this is the one stage where the understanding matters more than the circuit.

Done whenYou can negate a number using only parts you already own, and x + (-x) comes out zero.
2.06Needsall of Part I, 2.03–2.05

The ALU

ALU(x[16], y[16], zx, nx, zy, ny, f, no) → out[16], zr, ng

The arithmetic and logic unit: one box that computes any of a couple of dozen useful functions of two numbers, with control bits selecting which. Add, subtract, negate, increment, and, or, pass one input through, produce a constant — all from one chip.

Do not build a dozen circuits and mux between them. The whole menu comes from three phases: pre-condition each input, perform one operation on the results, then post-condition the answer. Six single-bit controls decide what happens at each phase, and they act in the order the numbers move through the chip.

zx — replace x with zero. nx — invert x, after any zeroing. zy and ny — the same two choices for y. f — the operation itself: 1 adds the two conditioned inputs, 0 ands them. no — invert the answer on the way out.

Every one of those six is a choice between two values, which is the chip you built in 1.05. So the ALU is layers of muxes with one adder and one and gate in the middle of them, and nothing in it is new.

Try the bits on paper before wiring anything. Set zx and zy and leave the rest at zero: both inputs become zero, and zero added to zero — or anded with it — is zero, so the chip produces the constant 0 without a constant anywhere in it. Then work out for yourself which settings give you x - y; it is a satisfying thing to find, and stage 2.05 is the hint.

Two extra outputs matter as much as the number itself. zr says the result was zero; ng says it was negative. These are the only things your machine will ever know about a computation, and every conditional in every program will be decided by them. Notice that ng is free — under two’s complement the sign is just the top bit — and that zr is the Or16Way from 1.08 with a Not on the end, which is why you built it.

Watch outThis is the largest chip so far and the most punishing to debug later. Test every control combination you intend to use, with at least one negative operand. An hour of test vectors here saves a week in Part IV.
III

Memory

Everything so far has been a function: inputs in, outputs out, no history. This Part breaks that. It is the conceptually hardest stretch of the whole build, and the most worthwhile.

3.01Needsa clear head

The leap into time

no new chip — an idea

Up to now every circuit has been a pure function of its inputs. Nothing you have built can answer “what was it a moment ago?” But a computer is nothing but a device for remembering things, so something has to change.

Here is the change: connect a gate’s output back to its own input. That is not a mistake and it is not a trick. Because a real gate takes time to respond, the value arriving back at the input is the value from before, and a circuit that can see its own past can hold a state. Memory is not a new kind of component. It is ordinary gates plus delay.

Try it on the bench before you build anything: wire one gate’s output back into its own input, switch the power on, and watch what happens. Then slow the clock right down and watch it again. The rest of this Part comes much easier once you have watched it happen.

Done whenYou can explain why feedback plus delay produces memory, without using the word “somehow”.
3.02Needs3.01

The latch

SRLatch(set, reset) → q

Pulse one input and the pair locks into one state; pulse the other and it locks into the opposite. Release both and it stays where it was. This is the first circuit you will build that remembers, and it is only two gates wide.

Build it from two NANDs, cross-coupled: each gate’s output goes into one input of the other. That spends one input on each gate and leaves one free, and those two free inputs are set and reset. Take q from either gate’s output; the other gate carries its opposite.

NAND is not an arbitrary pick, and it is worth seeing what the job demands. The gate has to invert, so that the loop’s two nodes hold opposite values — that pair of opposites is what gives the circuit two states to sit in rather than one, and it is exactly what the And in 3.01 could not provide. And it has to have a spare input, because that is the only way to reach in and command it; a Not inverts perfectly well but leaves you nowhere to put the instruction. Inverts, and has an input going spare: that is NAND. It is also Nor — an Or with a Not on the end — which gives you the same latch with every level turned upside down. Either is a real answer. Nothing else you have built is.

One consequence to have straight before you switch on: with NANDs the inputs are active low. Idle is 1, and you assert an input by pulling it to 0. That follows from the same property the latch is built on — a NAND with any input at 0 goes high whatever else is happening, so a 0 is the only thing that can overrule the loop, and a 1 leaves the gate listening to its partner. Which is precisely what idle ought to mean.

The right-hand end of that picture is the first of the latch’s two flaws: assert both inputs and it stops being a latch at all. q and its opposite go high together, which is not a stored bit, it is a contradiction — you have demanded it be one and zero at the same time. The second flaw is what happens when you leave that state — release both inputs together and the two gates are perfectly symmetric, with no way to choose. In this simulator that shows up honestly as an oscillation rather than as a convenient zero. Real hardware breaks the tie with manufacturing imperfection. You will break it with a reset.

Both flaws are why the next two stages exist.

Watch outIf the status bar says the circuit is oscillating, that may well be correct behaviour rather than a bug. Set one input before you switch on.
3.03Needs3.02

The gated latch

DLatch(d, enable) → q

One data input instead of two, plus an enable. While enable is high the output follows d; when enable drops, whatever was there is held. Now you have something you can actually store a bit in, and the contradictory-input problem is gone by construction.

One problem remains, and it is fatal for a computer: while enable is high this thing is transparent. Changes flow straight through it. If you chain two of them and enable both at once, a value races down the whole chain in one go instead of moving one step. A machine built from these would have no notion of “one step at a time”.

Done whenThe output tracks d while enabled and freezes when not.
3.04Needs3.03, the clock

The flip-flop

DFF(d, clk) → q

The most important idea in the sequential half of this build. A flip-flop samples its input at one instant — the moment the clock changes — and holds it steady for the rest of the cycle. Never transparent, ever.

Read the picture at the dashed lines and nowhere else. d moves seven times; q moves three, always at an edge, always to whatever d happened to be at that instant. What d does in between is not ignored by accident — it is the entire specification. This one samples on the clock’s falling edge; a rising-edge flip-flop is the same picture mirrored, and either is fine so long as the whole machine agrees on which.

The standard construction is two gated latches back to back, enabled on opposite halves of the clock, so that whichever one is listening, the other is holding. The value can only ever advance one stage per clock tick. That restriction is what makes the rest of the build tractable: it means the whole machine changes state together, in lockstep, and that every part of it can rely on the others staying still while it thinks.

When this works, connect a handful in a chain, feed the clock, and watch a single 1 march through them one stage per tick. That picture is worth keeping.

Watch outGive the clock a period comfortably longer than the deepest path of gates it drives, so the logic has time to settle before the next edge. Too fast a clock does not run your machine faster; it runs it wrongly.
3.05Needs3.04, 1.05

Bit

Bit(in, load, clk) → out

A flip-flop stores whatever arrives every single tick. What you actually want is storage that keeps its value until told otherwise. So: on each tick, store either the new input or the value already held — and choose between those two with a load signal.

Read that sentence once more. Choosing between two values with a control bit is a multiplexer, and the second value comes from the flip-flop’s own output. A one-bit register is a flip-flop with a mux in its feedback path, and that is the moment stage 1.05 pays for itself.

The middle edge is the one to look at. in has gone low and the clock has ticked, both of which would have moved a bare flip-flop; out does not move, because load was low and the mux was feeding the flip-flop its own output instead. Storage is not the flip-flop, which forgets what it holds every single tick. Storage is the flip-flop plus somewhere for the old value to go round again.

Done whenLoading a value sticks, and many ticks with load low leave it untouched.
3.06Needs3.05

Register

Register(in[16], load, clk) → out[16]

Sixteen Bits side by side, sharing one load signal and one clock. A whole number, remembered.

Mechanical, quick, and worth pausing over: this is the first component that would be recognisable to someone who has only ever seen a computer from the software side. It is a variable.

Done whenA sixteen-bit value survives a dozen ticks with load low, and changes on the tick you ask it to.
3.07Needs3.06, 1.09, 1.10

RAM8, and the pattern behind it

RAM8(in[16], load, address[3], clk) → out[16]

Eight registers, an address that selects one of them, and two jobs: route the write signal to exactly one register (that is your demux), and select one register’s output to present (that is your wide mux). Reading needs no clock; writing happens on the tick.

Build this one, and see the recursion: RAM64 is eight RAM8s addressed by three more bits. RAM512 is eight RAM64s. The structure never changes, only the depth of the address.

Then stop. Having proved you can, use the built-in RAM part for the real machine. It is worth saying why that is a reasonable place to stop: a sixteen-thousand-word memory made of flip-flops is around two and a half million gates, and real hardware does not build it that way either — an SRAM cell is six transistors, not a netlist of gates. You have demonstrated that memory can be made of NANDs. Now buy it off the shelf and spend your gates on the CPU, where the interesting decisions are.

Done whenWrites land at the right address, reads come back unchanged, and writing one address demonstrably leaves its neighbours alone.
3.08Needs3.06, 2.04, 1.05

Program counter

PC(in[16], load, inc, reset, clk) → out[16]

The register that says which instruction to run next, and the last piece of Part III. It needs exactly three behaviours, and each one corresponds to something a program does:

Increment — run the next instruction. This is what happens almost always. Load — jump to a given address, which is how you get if, while and function calls. Reset — go back to the beginning, which is what happens when you switch the machine on.

Three behaviours, one output: that is a register with a stack of muxes in front of it deciding what to store. You will have to settle which takes priority when more than one is asserted. Choose deliberately, write it down, and test it.

Done whenIt counts on its own, jumps when told, resets when told, and does the right thing when two controls are asserted at once.
IV

The machine

You now own every component a computer is made of. What remains is deciding what your computer is — and that is a design problem, not a wiring problem.

4.01Needspaper

Design the instruction set first

no new chip — a specification

Do not wire anything in this Part until you have written down your instruction set. The hardware is a consequence of the instruction set, not the other way round, and every hour spent here saves several later.

What you are deciding: how wide an instruction is, what an instruction can say, and how few kinds of instruction you can get away with. Fewer is dramatically better — every additional instruction format is more decoding logic and more ways to be wrong.

One proven shape, worth understanding before you invent your own: two instruction types, distinguished by a single bit. One loads a constant into a register. The other says “compute something, put the answer somewhere, and maybe jump” — with separate little fields for what to compute, where to put it, and when to jump. Two formats is enough to be a real computer. It is a small hinge for a large door.

Done whenYou have a written table: every bit of an instruction, what it means, and an example encoded by hand.
4.02Needs4.01

The memory map

no new chip — a decision

Decide what lives at which addresses, and write that down too. Some region is working memory. If you want a screen or a keyboard, they get addresses as well.

That last point is worth dwelling on. If a screen is simply a range of memory addresses whose contents happen to be displayed, then drawing a pixel is storing a number, and you need no new instructions, no new hardware path, and no concept of input or output at all. It is one of the tidiest economies in the design, and it costs nothing but a decision made now.

Done whenYou have an address map on paper, with a reason for each boundary.
4.03Needs3.06, 4.01

Two registers

no new chip — two Registers from 3.06

Your CPU needs somewhere to hold values it is working on. Two registers is enough, and each has a distinct character worth respecting.

One holds data — a number being worked on. The other does double duty: it holds either a value, or an address — which memory location to read or write, or which instruction to jump to. That double duty is what lets a machine with almost no instructions still reach any part of memory. To use a memory location, you first put its address in that register, then operate.

Sixteen bits of storage each, a load signal each. You already built these.

Done whenBoth registers hold values across ticks and load only when instructed.
4.04Needs4.01

The decoder is mostly wires

Decode(instruction[16]) → zx, nx, zy, ny, f, no, useM, loadA, loadD, writeM, jump[n]

“Instruction decoding” sounds like it needs a large and clever chip. With a well-chosen instruction set it needs almost nothing: the bits of the instruction are the control signals. The field that says what to compute is wired straight to the ALU’s control inputs. The field that says where to store the answer is wired straight to the load inputs of the registers.

The only real logic is a handful of gates deciding which instruction type you are looking at, and gating the control signals accordingly — because a constant-loading instruction must not accidentally trigger a memory write.

That list of outputs is not a choice. It is fixed by what your machine already has that needs controlling: the ALU’s six bits from 2.06, a load line for each of the two registers, whether memory is written, whether the ALU’s second input comes from memory or from the address register (useM), and the jump field passed on to 4.06. What is yours is which instruction bits produce them, and how wide the jump field is — the n in the signature is whatever your table in 4.01 says.

If this chip is turning out large, the problem is upstream in 4.01. Go back and simplify the encoding; that is much cheaper than building the decoder it demands.

Done whenEvery instruction bit has a named destination, and you can point at where each one goes.
4.05Needs2.06, 4.03, 4.04

The datapath

CPU(instruction[16], inM[16], clk) → outM[16], writeM, addressM[15], pc[15]

Now assemble the CPU. This is the largest chip you will draw. It can look like too much at first; it helps that everything in it is a part you have already built and tested.

The structure is a loop. The ALU takes two inputs — one always from the data register, the other selected between the address register and a value fetched from memory. Its output goes back to the registers and out to memory. Which of those actually happen is decided by the control bits from 4.04. Every one of those choices is a mux; every mux is stage 1.05.

Work in the order the signal flows and test as you go: get the ALU fed correctly first, then get its output stored correctly, then worry about jumps.

Watch outThe single most common bug here is a value being read in the same tick it is written. Sketch on paper what is stable when. This is exactly the discipline the flip-flop bought you in 3.04 — use it.
4.06Needs4.05, 3.08

Jumps

Jump(zr, ng, jump[n]) → load

The last piece of the CPU, and the one that turns a calculator into a computer. Your ALU reports two facts about its result: it was zero (zr) and it was negative (ng). Your instruction carries a small field saying which outcomes should cause a jump. Compare the two, and if they agree, tell the program counter to load instead of increment.

That handful of gates is where every if, every while, every function call and every loop in every program that will ever run on this machine comes from. It is worth a moment of respect.

Done whenEach jump condition fires exactly when it should, tested with a positive, a negative and a zero result.
4.07Needs4.02

Talking to the world

no new chip — a region of RAM that something else is watching

Optional, and worth it. If you reserved addresses for a screen and a keyboard back in 4.02, wire them up now — a region of memory whose contents are displayed, and a location that holds whichever key is currently down.

Notice that the CPU needs no changes whatsoever. It has no idea a screen exists. It stores a number at an address, exactly as it always does, and something else is watching. Getting output out of a machine you built from one gate is a good day.

Done whenStoring to a display address changes something you can see.
4.08Needs4.05, 4.06, 3.07

The computer

Computer(reset, clk) → nothing — the output is what it leaves in memory

Three boxes and the wires between them: a ROM holding the program, the CPU, and the RAM. The counter’s output addresses the ROM; the ROM’s output is the instruction; the CPU’s memory outputs go to the RAM and back.

Keeping instructions and data in separate memories is a real architectural choice, called Harvard, and what it buys you is simplicity: the machine can fetch an instruction and touch data in the same tick without them queuing for the same memory. The cost is that a program cannot modify itself, which is rarely a loss at this scale.

Wire it up. Then stop, because you have built a computer, and there is nothing left to add to the hardware. Everything after this is software.

Done whenYou can load a program, press reset, run the clock, and watch the counter advance through it.
V

Software

The hardware is finished and will not change again. Everything from here is built on top of it, in layers, each one making the next one bearable.

5.01Needs4.08

A program in binary

your instruction table → bits in the ROM, by hand

Write something tiny directly in machine code — add two numbers and store the result — encode it by hand from your table in 4.01, load it into the ROM, and run it.

Do this exactly once. It is the moment the hardware stops being a diagram and becomes a machine, and it will teach you more about your instruction set in ten minutes than an afternoon of staring at it. It is also the quickest way to see why you want an assembler, which is the point.

Done whenYou single-step through your own machine code and the right number lands in the right place.
5.02Needs5.01

The assembler

text → machine code

A program that turns readable mnemonics into the bits you just encoded by hand. Write it in whatever language you like; it runs on your own computer, not on the one you built.

Two features earn their keep immediately: symbolic names for instructions, and labels for addresses, so that a jump target is @loop and not the number 47 — a number that changes the moment you insert a line. Everything else is optional.

This is also the first opportunity to write a real test: assemble the program from 5.01 and check you get the same bits you produced by hand.

Done whenA labelled loop assembles and runs correctly on the hardware.
5.03Needs5.02

A stack, and a convention for calling things

a region of RAM, plus rules → functions that can call functions

Functions need somewhere to keep their local variables and their return address, and it must nest — a function can call a function that calls a function. A stack is the structure that does this, and it is not hardware: it is a region of RAM plus a register holding a pointer to the top of it, plus a set of rules everyone agrees to follow.

Design the rules deliberately and write them down: who pushes the arguments, who saves what, where the return value lives, who cleans up. This is the bridge between a machine that runs instructions and a machine that runs programs, and almost every hard bug in the rest of the project will be a violation of these rules.

Done whenA function calls another function, both return correctly, and recursion to a depth of ten does not corrupt anything.
5.04Needs5.03

An intermediate language

stack operations → assembly

A small stack-based language — push, pop, add, compare, call, return — and a translator from it into your assembly.

Skipping this layer is a common way for the project to stall. Compiling a high-level language directly to machine code means every language feature has to be solved in the same breath as every hardware detail, and the two problems tangle. This layer cuts the knot: the compiler above it only has to think about meaning, and this translator only has to think about your CPU. Each half becomes something you can hold in your head.

Done whenA hand-written program in your intermediate language translates, assembles, and runs.
5.05Needs5.04

The compiler

your language → the intermediate language

Now design a language you would actually enjoy writing, and compile it down one level. Variables, expressions, if, while, functions, and some way to group data together. That is enough for real programs; resist adding more until you have run something.

Because you are targeting the intermediate language rather than the metal, each construct becomes a small, local translation — a while is a couple of labels and a conditional jump, an expression is a walk over its own tree emitting pushes. That keeps each construct small enough to hold in your head.

Done whenA program with a loop and a function call compiles all the way down and runs on your hardware.
5.06Needs5.05

A runtime

your language → multiply, divide, allocate, draw

Your hardware can add. It cannot multiply, divide, allocate memory or draw a line. Those are library functions, written in your own language, and this is the layer people forget to budget for.

Multiplication is repeated addition until you make it cleverer. Division is the same in reverse. Memory allocation is bookkeeping over a region of RAM. Each one is a small program, and writing them is the first time you get to use the thing you built rather than build it.

Done whenMultiply and divide are correct, including for negative numbers and zero.
5.07Needs5.06

Primes

your language → the primes below a thousand

Write a sieve, in your language, and print the primes below a thousand.

When it runs, trace what actually happened. A number you typed was compiled to a stack language, translated to assembly, assembled into bits, stored in a ROM you configured, fetched by a counter you designed, decoded by wires you placed, and computed by an arithmetic unit that is, at the bottom, some thousands of copies of a single gate that only knows how to say “not both”.

There is no part of that chain you took on trust.

Appendix: working practices

Four things that are not stages but will decide how the build goes.

A.1

When it will not settle

A circuit that reports oscillating is telling you something specific: some loop of gates has no stable answer. Three usual causes. You have accidental feedback — a signal reaching backwards into something that feeds it. You have a genuine latch that started symmetric, in which case it is behaving correctly and needs a defined initial state. Or your clock is faster than the logic it drives, so the next edge arrives before the last one has finished propagating.

Slow the clock right down first. Most of the time the answer becomes obvious the moment you can watch it happen.

A.2

Debugging by bisection

When a large chip misbehaves, do not read the whole diagram. Put a probe halfway along and ask one question: is the value here already wrong? That halves the search. Repeat. Six probes will find a fault in a chip with sixty components.

If the halfway point is correct and the output is wrong, you have eliminated half your circuit without understanding any of it. That is the whole trick, and it works just as well on the compiler in Part V as on the ALU in Part II.

A.3

Library hygiene

Name parts after what they do, not how they work: Mux16, not BigSelectThing. Group them into folders by Part as you go, so the list stays navigable when it reaches forty entries.

When you improve a chip, edit it in place rather than making Adder2 — every circuit using it picks up the fix. And if you do end up with a genuinely better rewrite alongside the original, swap the old one out wholesale rather than rewiring by hand; that way the tool can tell you which connections will not survive before anything breaks.

A.4

When you are stuck

Three questions, in order. What exactly should this part do? — write the truth table before the circuit; half the time the answer appears while you write it. What do I already have? — the piece you need has usually been built already, two stages ago, under a different name. Can I say it differently? — “a or b” became buildable the moment it was restated as “not both off”, and that move works far more often than it has any right to.

And if none of that helps: build the smaller thing. A part that resists you is nearly always two parts that have not been separated yet.

One last thing

This will probably take longer than you expect, and the stretch between 2.06 and 3.04 is the one to watch: it is where the ideas are densest. Past it, the rest builds momentum.

Test everything. Name everything. Never build the same part twice.
